☀️ Big Story of the Day
Canada's population growth just fell to its slowest rate since World War I — and it's the real reason the housing market went quiet

StatCan released new numbers this week showing Canada's population grew just 0.5% in the year ending July 1, 2026 — the slowest percentage growth since 1915/1916, during World War I (StatCan, Canadian Mortgage Trends). The population is still growing — it hit 41.8 million — but the pace has cratered because non-permanent residents (people here on study or work permits) dropped by about 154,600, with study permit holders down over 140,000 and total immigration admissions down 4.2% year-over-year (Global News).

Here's the part that matters for this audience: BMO senior economist Robert Kavcic connected the dots directly, saying "the housing sector in particular has felt the impact from lower immigration levels, with both sales activity and home prices stalling" (Canadian Mortgage Trends). Fewer new people means fewer new renters and buyers — that's a bigger driver of today's slow market than any single interest rate move.

Why it's video-worthy: a "slowest since World War I" comparison is the kind of stat that stops a scroll even for people who don't follow housing news, and it directly explains the market Dmytro's audience is living in — plus it's personal for anyone who came to Canada on a study or work permit, since they're the ones the policy pullback is aimed at.

🎯 The One Thing
New Bank of Canada research says most Canadian households are quietly going into debt every year just to keep up with inflation — even ones that look "fine."

A Bank of Canada researcher broke down five years of household income versus spending data and found the "average" $274 surplus Canadians ran in 2025 hides a much rougher reality underneath (Better Dwelling). The bottom 60% of households are actually running an annual shortfall — the middle-income (2nd) quintile is down $6,805 a year, the hardest hit. By age, everyone under 35 is in the red, averaging a $4,249 annual shortfall — the only age group with negative cashflow. Even homeowners as a group are running a $1,817/year shortfall, while renters are close to breakeven at −$90. Inflation — which sat at 3.0% in the latest StatCan print, still above the Bank's 2% target — "does not erase the effects of higher prices" already baked into people's budgets (StatCan). Translation: wages went up on paper, but for most people, especially under 35, real life still costs more than they're bringing in — so the gap gets covered with debt.

🧠 Why It Matters (To You)

If you're a buyer or a first-timer: fewer newcomers competing for homes plus condo sales at a 10-year low means genuinely less competition than the last few years — this is closer to a buyer's market than anything we've had in a while. If you're a seller or you bought near the top: prices settling back toward 2021 levels is painful, but it's the market correcting, not something you did wrong.

If you're under 35, or you just don't feel like your paycheque stretches as far as it should: the Bank of Canada's own numbers say you're not imagining it — most people your age are running an actual annual shortfall right now, not just feeling tight.

And if you came to Canada on a study or work permit, or you're the parent of someone who did: this week's population data is really about people like you — the slowdown in newcomers is a big piece of why the market and the economy both feel different than they did two years ago.
