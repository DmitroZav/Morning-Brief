☀️ Big Story of the Day
15 days to the 50% tariff wall — and now the auto industry is in the crossfire

Markets reopened this morning after the long weekend, and the clock didn't stop over the holiday. In 15 days — August 19 — a second, bigger wave of US tariffs is scheduled to hit: 50% on a list of Canadian goods. A tariff is just a tax on things crossing the border, paid by the importer and almost always passed on to the shopper. Desjardins, one of Canada's big financial groups, now pegs the goods caught in this fight at about $28 billion a year — roughly 5% of everything we sell to the US. The list hits lumber, cement, furniture, clothing, appliances, wine and dairy.

The fresh worry today is jobs. One of the most-watched mortgage voices in the country warned this morning that "Canada could lose its entire auto industry" — because on top of the Aug 19 list, our carmakers are already carrying separate US tariffs, and every round of this fight chips at the factories that employ tens of thousands of people in Ontario. The one steadying note: Prime Minister Mark Carney and President Trump have agreed to intensify talks in the run-up to the deadline, and Carney is openly hinting the whole Aug 19 threat may be a negotiating deadline rather than a certainty — "normally there's a deadline, normally there's an outsized tariff attached to it."

Why it's video-worthy: everyone else is still doing generic "tariff panic." You get to be the calm one with the calendar: here's the ONE date (Aug 19), here's the ONE item on the list that touches your future home (lumber and cement), and here's why the auto-jobs angle matters even if you never buy a car. Specific, honest, useful — the post people send to their family group chat.

🎯 The One Thing
Toronto condo prices just slid about $60,000 — and it's not mainly interest rates. It's oversupply, and it's handing buyers the most leverage they've had in years.

Here's the plain version. For a couple of years builders threw up small, investor-style condos — tiny units meant to be flipped or rented, not lived in. Now those investors have pulled back, finished units are sitting empty, and prices are giving way. A typical Toronto condo is down roughly $60,000, and the reason is simple supply and demand: too many units, not enough buyers. Interest rates are part of it, but the bigger driver is the glut itself. For anyone trying to buy their first place, that's not scary news — it's the door opening. Sellers who are bleeding fees on an empty unit want out, and that's where your negotiating power lives. This is idea 1 below.

🧠 Why It Matters (To You)

If you're a first-time buyer or newcomer: the condo market is the softest, most buyer-friendly corner in years. A typical unit is down about $60,000, thousands sit empty, and sellers are competing for you. Your leverage today is on price and choice — negotiate hard, ask for incentives, take your time. Just know the flip side: condo building has stalled (starts down ~11%), so this window of oversupply won't stay open forever.

If you're a renter: the empty-condo glut is quietly on your side — landlords are competing for tenants and rents have cooled. If your lease is up, shop and negotiate instead of auto-renewing. Longer term, watch Aug 19: if tariffs tax lumber and cement, building gets pricier, fewer homes get built, and tighter supply eventually nudges rents back up.

If you earn a paycheque in Ontario: the tariff fight isn't just abstract politics. The auto sector employs tens of thousands here, and the Aug 19 wave plus the separate car tariffs put real jobs on the line. A weaker jobs market cools home demand — good if you're bargain-hunting, painful if it's your industry. Watch the deadline and watch the talks.
