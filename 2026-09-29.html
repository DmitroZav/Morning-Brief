☀️ Big Story of the Day
The US just banned Canadian beer, wine, spirits and big motorcycles — and slapped 50% tariffs on our cheese, paper, aluminum and furniture

Just after midnight on Sept 29, a set of US import bans on Canadian goods took effect: beer, wine and spirits, whey (a dairy by-product), molasses, and motorcycles with engines over 800cc. Cheese, paper, aluminum, wood, furniture and lighting now face 50% US tariffs instead of an outright ban. One estimate puts about US$19.9 billion of Canadian exports in the crosshairs, small next to roughly $715 billion of yearly cross-border trade, but a trade lawyer notes "a ban is usually here to stay," which makes it harder to undo than a tariff (Bloomberg, Traders Agency summary, CNBC).

Ottawa's reply: Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc called the measures "illegal and unjustified" but signalled he is open to talks. Canada has had its own counter-tariffs of 15–50% on C$27.6 billion of US goods since Sept 8. Trump says he expects a trade deal "within weeks" and expects Canada to apologize (CTV News). Same day, the union told CTV that Stelco will lay off 350 workers at its Hamilton plant, with a US analyst tying it to the tariffs (CTV News; layoffs reported to start Oct 9, CTV video).

Why it's video-worthy: it is the first time the trade war shows up as a named list of products AND a named Ontario layoff in the same 24 hours. Your viewers can point at it: the LCBO shelf, the Hamilton steel plant, the paycheque.

🎯 The One Thing
Canadian mortgage arrears are up 96% from their 2022 low: 14,270 mortgages are behind on payments, the most since the 2014 condo glut.

The Canadian Bankers Association data (July 2026), reported by Better Dwelling: 14,270 mortgages in arrears (meaning the borrower is behind on payments), up 24.9% from a year ago and 96.2% above the August 2022 low. The arrears rate is 0.29%, up 6 basis points year over year and back to levels last seen in 2014, when Toronto and Vancouver had a condo glut. Banks' total mortgage count is 4.91 million, a 6-year low.

Honest context: 0.29% is still about 3 in every 1,000 mortgages. The scary part is the direction, not the level. Better Dwelling's read is that households are tapping credit to cover costs while prices outpace wages; that is their interpretation, not a bank quote. No Ontario/GTA breakdown in the source.

🧠 Why It Matters (To You)

If you work in manufacturing, steel, dairy, drinks or trucking in Ontario: this is the first week the trade war has a specific product list. Jobs are what pay the mortgage, so watch your employer's US exposure.

If you're buying: a weaker job picture plus more people behind on payments usually means more cautious buyers and more negotiating room. Not a crash signal, just a reason not to stretch your budget to the limit.

If you're a newcomer: the trade war reaches you through prices and job openings, not politics. If your income is tied to a trade-hit industry, build a bigger cash cushion before signing a lease or mortgage.
