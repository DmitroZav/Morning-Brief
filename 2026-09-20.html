☀️ Big Story of the Day
A Quebec party just said it'll try to hold a $130-million vote on leaving Canada — even without a majority.

Quebec votes for a new government on October 5th, and the Parti Québécois (PQ) — a party that wants Quebec to separate from Canada — is leading in every recent poll: 29% in a Leger survey right after last week's debate, ahead of the governing CAQ at 24%, the Liberals at 22%, the Conservatives at 15% and Québec Solidaire at 10% (Leger). PQ leader Paul St-Pierre Plamondon has now confirmed his referendum push doesn't depend on winning a majority — "whether we're in a minority or majority, that commitment doesn't change," he said this week, and his own party has priced the vote at a minimum of $130 million (CTV News, CBC News). The final leaders' debate is Wednesday, September 23rd (Wikipedia).

Why it's video-worthy: "a province might vote to spend $130 million trying to leave the country" is the kind of headline that grabs anyone, political-news-follower or not — and it's a live, developing story with two weeks left before the vote. No direct wallet impact for a Toronto-based viewer today, so it leads the Big Story slot rather than taking a full video — see "Why These Made the Cut" below.

🎯 The One Thing
Diesel just hit an all-time record high in Canada — and experts say your grocery bill is the next thing to feel it.

Diesel is the fuel that moves almost everything in this country — the trucks that stock grocery stores, the trains, the farm equipment bringing in this fall's harvest. It just hit a record $2.751/litre nationally, driven by Middle East conflict disruptions and global refinery shortages (CTV News). "It's almost immediate" how this hits Canadians' wallets, per industry experts — the Canada Truck Operators Association says small and mid-sized carriers are under the most strain right now, and those extra shipping costs get passed straight into the price of food and goods on store shelves, typically within 30 to 60 days (RealAgriculture). Translation: nobody needs to drive a diesel truck to feel this one.

Hook: There are three seller debt traps nobody warns home buyers about — and one of them almost got past me too.

Body: When you buy a home, you're not just buying the property — you can inherit problems tied to it if nobody catches them first. A seller's unpaid property taxes, unregistered liens from unpaid contractors, or old mortgage discharges that were never properly filed can all quietly follow the home into your name. In Ontario, your lawyer is supposed to catch these during the title search before closing — but "supposed to" isn't the same as "always does," especially with a lawyer juggling a dozen files. This matters double if you're new to Canada: you don't yet have the instinct for which questions to ask, and you're trusting a system you've never used before. The fix isn't complicated — it's asking specific questions and reading your title search results yourself, not just trusting a summary email.

Hook: I bought a condo near the peak and felt this exact pain — here's why some Toronto condos are losing value while others hold steady.

Body: Not every condo is created equal, and treating "buy a condo" as a blanket investment strategy is how a lot of buyers, including me at one point, end up disappointed. The units losing the most value tend to share a few things in common: they're small studio or micro-units competing against a flood of nearly identical new listings, they're in buildings with rising maintenance fees and no distinguishing features, or they were bought purely on the promise of future appreciation rather than because someone actually wanted to live there. With so much unsold inventory sitting on the market right now, buildings without something that makes them stand out are the ones sitting the longest and losing value the fastest. The lesson isn't "don't buy a condo" — it's "know exactly what you're buying and why," instead of assuming any condo in Toronto is automatically a safe bet.
