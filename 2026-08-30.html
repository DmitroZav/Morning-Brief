☀️ Big Story of the Day
Teachers and parents rallied across Ontario Saturday — and the contract that decides if school starts on time expires Monday.

Thousands of teachers, education workers and parents held rallies in dozens of Ontario municipalities on Saturday, protesting how the province is funding schools — cut librarian and vice-principal positions, reduced special-education support — right as the collective agreements covering Ontario's education unions are set to expire this Monday, August 31, just before students go back to school. The province's side: Education Minister Paul Calandra's office points to $30.6 billion in "core education funding" this year and says it remains "committed to a negotiated settlement." The unions' side: organizers say classrooms don't have the staff or supplies they need, and one Toronto MPP warned stalled talks could lead to strike action right as the school year starts. Neither side has confirmed a deal (CBC News, Aug 29).

Why it's video-worthy: even if you don't follow Ontario politics, "will school start on time this week" is a question every parent — especially a newcomer family still deciding which city or school board to settle near — actually needs answered. 🎯 The One Thing
Mortgage arrears "improved" for the first time in over a year — but it was 40 mortgages, and banks are quietly holding the fewest mortgages in almost 6 years.

Every month, Canada's biggest banks report how many mortgages are "in arrears" — meaning 90+ days behind on payments — through the Canadian Bankers Association. In June, that rate ticked down for the first time since March 2025, from 0.285% to 0.284%. Sounds like good news. Except the entire "improvement" was just 40 fewer mortgages in arrears — 14,021 instead of 14,061 — out of nearly 5 million mortgages on the books. That's a rounding blip, not a recovery: arrears are still up 25% year-over-year (Better Dwelling, Aug 27). The part that didn't make headlines: the total number of mortgages Canadian banks hold fell for the ninth month in a row, to its lowest level since October 2020 — almost 6 years. Banks may not be fixing shaky mortgages so much as declining to renew them, pushing those borrowers toward pricier "B lenders" instead. If you've got a renewal coming up in the next year or two, that's the number that should actually get your attention — not the misleading "improvement" headline.

🧠 Why It Matters (To You)

Anyone renewing or refinancing a mortgage in the next year or two: don't read the "arrears improved" headline as relief. Banks holding fewer mortgages overall is a bigger warning sign — it can mean lenders are getting pickier about who they renew, not that borrowers are suddenly in better shape.

First-time buyer or newcomer building your finances from zero: new StatCan data proves the math genuinely got harder, not just in Toronto or Vancouver — even in provinces marketed as "affordable" like Nova Scotia, first-time buyers now earn over a quarter more than the typical household. That's useful, not discouraging: it tells you the real income target instead of leaving you guessing.

Parents choosing where to settle in the GTA: Monday's contract deadline for Ontario's teachers is worth watching before you lock in a neighbourhood — a strike right at back-to-school would affect real families this week, regardless of your politics.
