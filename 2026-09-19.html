☀️ Big Story of the Day
Canada's PM just flew to a tiny French island 20km off Newfoundland — and it's about way more than geography.

This weekend, Prime Minister Mark Carney is meeting French President Emmanuel Macron on Saint-Pierre-et-Miquelon — three small islands, home to under 6,000 people, that belong to France and sit just off Newfoundland's south coast (Wikipedia). It's the first time a Canadian PM has ever made an official visit there, and the first visit by a French president in over a decade (Globe and Mail). The two leaders are talking aerospace, energy, critical minerals, quantum tech, satellites and even turning the islands into a "rear base" for Arctic cooperation with Denmark and Greenland (France 24, CBC News).

Why it's video-worthy: "France has islands 20 minutes from Newfoundland" is the kind of surprising-trivia hook that stops the scroll on its own — and it's backed by a real, bigger story: Canada visibly leaning toward Europe while things stay tense with the US (PM's Office). No direct wallet impact today, so it leads the Big Story slot rather than a full video — see "Why These Made the Cut" below.

🎯 The One Thing
CMHC just quietly moved the finish line on what counts as "affordable" housing — without a single new home being built.

CMHC's Fall 2026 Housing Supply Report changed its own target: instead of aiming to bring prices back down to 2004-level affordability, the agency now says restoring 2019 ("pre-pandemic") affordability by 2036 is the goal — a much easier bar, since prices were already far higher by 2019 (Better Dwelling, CMHC). Even with the softer target, Canada would need 417,000–469,000 new homes built every year through 2036 — but the country is only on pace for about 229,000–247,000 (Canadian Mortgage Trends). Translation: the government's own definition of "success" got easier to hit on paper, while the actual homes still aren't showing up.

🧠 Why It Matters (To You)

If you're a first-time buyer trying to time the market: the government just admitted, in its own numbers, that it isn't close to either affordability target — old or new — so "wait for it to get truly affordable" could mean waiting a decade or more; make your decision on your own budget, not on a government promise. If you're renting or house-hunting anywhere near a big city: the reason new supply is so slow to show up isn't just interest rates — it's 50-year-old farmland-protection laws in BC, Quebec and Ontario that quietly capped where cities can grow, and that's a much harder problem to fix than a rate cut. If you're a newcomer: pay attention to Alberta's Oct 19 referendum even if you live in Ontario — a question about tying provincial services to immigration status is exactly the kind of policy debate that can spread to other provinces once it's on the table.

Script

Hook: There's a law from the 1970s still quietly deciding how many homes get built in Canada today.

Body: Back in the 1970s, BC created the Agricultural Land Reserve, Quebec set up its CPTAQ zoning rules, and Ontario later built out the Greenbelt — all with a good goal: protect farmland from being paved over. But those laws also drew a permanent, invisible boundary around our biggest, fastest-growing cities. Fifty years later, that boundary is a huge reason cities can't just expand outward to build more homes — the land right next to Toronto, Vancouver and Montreal is legally locked up for farming, whether or not it's actually being farmed. That land squeeze feeds land speculation and pushes prices up on whatever's left inside the boundary. This is a big piece of why "just build more homes" is way harder than it sounds — it's not only about interest rates or investors, it's about laws written before most of us were born.
