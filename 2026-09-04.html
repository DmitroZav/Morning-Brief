☀️ Big Story of the Day
Canada just bet $4.7 billion that it can build its own trains again.

Yesterday in Thunder Bay, Prime Minister Mark Carney announced that for the first time in four decades, VIA Rail's passenger cars will actually be built in Canada. The federal government is putting more than $4.7 billion toward 313 new rail cars from Alstom Canada, manufactured in Thunder Bay, Ontario and La Pocatière, Québec (Prime Minister of Canada, Sept 3). Up to now, a lot of VIA Rail's fleet was built in the United States. This is the "Buy Canadian Policy" in action — Canadian steel, Canadian companies, Canadian workers — and it lands nearly 700 jobs directly in Ontario and Québec, plus work for over 900 Canadian suppliers. Combined with an earlier $1.95 billion investment in new locomotives announced in July, that's $6.6 billion going into Canadian-built trains total.

Why it's video-worthy: it's a genuinely positive, easy-to-explain story in the middle of a trade war people are anxious about — proof the response to tariffs isn't only "tax them back," it's also "build it here." Zero jargon, easy for newcomers to grab onto, and it's never been covered on this account before.

🎯 The One Thing
Canada's hot job market just stalled hard — the economy lost 42,000 jobs in August, and that's the first real hint the Bank of Canada might actually cut rates.

Statistics Canada released the Labour Force Survey for August this morning (StatCan, Sept 4; reported via BNN Bloomberg/The Canadian Press). Economists were expecting a gain of roughly 15,000 jobs. Instead, Canada lost 42,000 — a huge miss. The unemployment rate held at 6.4%, but that's mostly because people stopped actively job-hunting, not because things improved. This snaps a hot streak: from April through July, the economy had added 181,000 jobs and unemployment had been falling. The public sector shed jobs for a third straight month (−20,000 in August), and workers aged 15–24 lost 19,000 positions. One bright spot: manufacturing actually gained 22,000 jobs despite the US tariffs — a surprise pocket of strength. Crucially, this data barely captures the impact of the new 50% US tariffs that hit mid-August, so next month's report could look worse. For anyone waiting on a fixed or variable rate to drop, a weakening job market is exactly the kind of signal that nudges the Bank of Canada toward a cut.
