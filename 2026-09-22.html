☀️ Big Story of the Day
Quebec's separatist party is leading every poll — but most of its own voters don't actually want the referendum it's promising.

The Parti Québécois sits at roughly 28% in the polls heading into the October 5th election, comfortably ahead of the pack, and its leader Paul St-Pierre Plamondon has already said a sovereignty referendum will happen even without a majority government, with a price tag of at least $130 million (Pallas Data, Sept 21). But a poll reported yesterday shows support for actual independence stuck around 30% province-wide — and only 55% of people planning to vote PQ say they'd vote yes in a referendum themselves (CBC News, Sept 21).

Why it's video-worthy: "the party about to win doesn't even have its own voters convinced" is a contrarian twist that works whether or not someone follows Quebec politics closely — and it's the kind of political uncertainty that quietly touches the loonie and investor confidence in the rest of Canada.

🎯 The One Thing
Canada's ban on foreign home buyers expires in 100 days — and right now, nobody's extended it.

The Prohibition on the Purchase of Residential Property by Non-Canadians Act, in place since 2023 and already extended once, is set to expire January 1, 2027 (CMHC). As of this week, Ottawa hasn't announced another extension, a repeal, or a replacement — though the housing minister has floated a narrower, Australia-style model instead of a blanket ban (Deeded.ca). A creator on the watchlist flagged the same countdown today — see Creator Watch below.

🧠 Why It Matters (To You)

If you're a newcomer without citizenship or PR yet: the foreign buyer ban has been keeping you (and a lot of other non-Canadians) out of the resale market since 2023 — that countdown clock is now under 100 days, and how it plays out could change whether you're allowed to buy a home here at all next year. If you're buying or selling in the GTA this fall: the "wait for the right season" advice matters less than the actual numbers right now — the national sales-to-listings ratio just hit its weakest reading for any August since 1997, which in plain terms means there's more room to negotiate than the calendar alone would suggest. If you just want to understand what's happening around you: a Quebec government that might not have a majority is still promising a referendum most Quebecers — including most of its own supporters — don't actually want, and that kind of political uncertainty is exactly the sort of thing that quietly moves the loonie and the interest rates you pay, even if you never vote in a Quebec election.
