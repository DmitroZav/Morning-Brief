BOND YIELD HITS FRESH HIGH 3.47% · DOW -628 PTS · CAD $0.726 USD
☀️ Big Story of the Day
Wall Street just had its worst day in weeks — and Canada's trade war is the reason.

The day Canada's counter-tariffs actually took effect, US markets sold off hard: the Dow Jones fell 628 points (−1.18%) to close at 52,786, the S&P 500 dropped 0.58%, and the Nasdaq slipped 0.32% (Investing.com). Investors are pricing in disrupted North American supply chains — steel, aluminum, autos, and copper are all deeply tangled between the two countries, and copper futures actually hit an all-time high on tariff fears while broader markets fell. The Canadian dollar swung too, briefly touching its weakest level since August 19 during the session before steadying (Investing.com).

Why it's video-worthy: this is the first time the trade war has shown up as an actual number in people's investment accounts rather than a policy headline — and most Canadians don't realize their RRSP or TFSA has US stock exposure even if they never bought a single American company directly.

🎯 The One Thing
Canada's bond yield — the number that actually sets your fixed mortgage rate — just hit a fresh high, right as most new mortgages are betting rates will fall.

The 5-year Government of Canada bond yield closed at 3.47% today, up from the roughly 3.35–3.42% range it's held for weeks and the highest level we've tracked in this brief all year (Trading Economics). This is the number lenders actually watch to price fixed mortgages — not the Bank of Canada's overnight rate. At the same time, a new CMHC report shows more than 85% of new uninsured mortgages in early 2026 were either variable-rate (35.5%) or fixed for less than 5 years (49.5%) — meaning most people signing a mortgage today are quietly betting that rates come down before their term is up (Canadian Mortgage Trends / CMHC). Today's yield spike is a reminder that bet isn't guaranteed to pay off.

🧭 Why These Made the Cut · Fresh vs. Recent

Why these beat the other candidates: the bond yield hitting a fresh high on the same week CMHC flagged record numbers of Canadians choosing variable and short-term mortgages is a genuinely new combination — real-time market data plus a structural warning nobody's covered yet — so it takes the One Thing and top video slot. The Wall Street selloff is the sharpest, most nationally-significant story of the day and the clearest "trade war meets your wallet" moment since tariffs actually landed, so it leads as Big Story. Property Daily's condo-fees video posted just hours ago and is the strongest STEAL: a brand-new topic for this brief, directly useful for anyone buying into the GTA's softest segment.

Fresh vs. recent: tariffs have been the throughline for three straight days (Sept 7 currency threat, Sept 8 implementation + Carney's address). Today deliberately does not lead with "tariffs are live" again — the trade war only appears today as market fallout (stocks, the loonie), while the real estate lead moves to mortgage-market structure, a data point never used in this brief before. Condo fees are also a first for this brief.

🧠 Why It Matters (To You)

If you're shopping for a mortgage right now: the safest read of today is "don't assume rates are about to fall" — the bond market, the thing that actually prices fixed mortgages, just moved the opposite direction. If you already have a variable or short-term mortgage (and odds are high you do — 85% of new borrowers now do), today's data is a nudge to build a buffer into your budget rather than count on renewal being cheaper. If you have a TFSA, RRSP, or workplace pension: yesterday's market selloff likely touched it even if you never bought a US stock yourself — most Canadian retirement funds hold American companies. If you're buying a condo: Property Daily's video today is worth ten minutes — condo fees are climbing for reasons that have nothing to do with your unit getting nicer, and it changes how you should read a status certificate before you buy.

A video that came out yesterday broke down where that money actually goes, and it's worth knowing before you buy, not after. Part of it is normal inflation — insurance, cleaning, landscaping all cost more than they did a few years ago. But the bigger driver is the reserve fund: the pool of money every building sets aside for major repairs, like a roof, elevators, or the parking garage. A lot of buildings underfunded their reserve for years, and now aging construction plus higher repair costs are forcing boards to catch up fast, which means bigger fee increases or special assessments — a one-time bill on top of your regular fees. Before you buy any condo, ask for the status certificate and actually read the reserve fund study, not just the monthly fee number. A building with a low fee today but an underfunded reserve can cost you more in a surprise assessment than a building with a slightly higher fee that's properly funded."
